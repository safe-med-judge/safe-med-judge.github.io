01 The position
LLJ–expert alignment should not be reported as standalone evidence of validity or safety.

LLM-as-judges (LLJs) are increasingly used to scale medical AI evaluation and cut clinician review costs. This creates a second-order safety problem: medical AI systems may be benchmarked, selected, or trusted based on judgments produced by other models.

What studies typically report

What agreement alone does not establish

  • Reliability

    The same verdict across prompts, repeated runs, and judge models.

  • Clinical validity

    Judgments grounded in clinical evidence and task-specific medical rules.

  • Safety

    Fitness for the downstream decision the score is used to make.

Why medicine needs a stricter standard than general NLG

Open-ended

Notes, answers, and treatment plans are free text, not a single option to match.

Context-dependent

Whether an output is acceptable depends on the patient, the source record, and the setting.

Safety-critical

A judge error can pass an unsafe answer, reward unsupported content, or reject a valid diagnosis.

No single ground truth

Evidence can be incomplete and criteria ambiguous, so clinicians themselves may disagree.

One sentence of system prompt flips the verdict

GPT-5.6 Luna decides whether a predicted diagnosis matches the gold standard (MedCaseReasoning prompt).

System prompt: “You are a professional medical expert that answers only ‘y’ or ‘n’.”

  • Dx1GoldUrticaria multiformeDx1PredAcute urticaria
    Subtype vs. broader diagnosis: acceptance depends on whether the task permits broader diagnoses.
  • Dx2GoldOculomotor mononeuropathyDx2PredPost-vaccinal acute oculomotor nerve palsy
    Same ICD-10 category (H49.0), but post-vaccinal palsy is one rare cause — not another name for it.
  • Dx3GoldCholecystitisDx3PredPerforated gallbladder
    Accepted by the MIMIC-CDM benchmark rule, yet rejected by the judge either way.

A more detailed prompt with alias examples fixes Dx1 and Dx2, but Dx3 is still labeled incorrectly: a judge can be consistently wrong when the prompt does not encode task-specific medical rules.

Takeaway High accuracy under a single judge–prompt configuration does not establish reliable medical evaluation; the judge, prompt, and scoring rules should be validated together, with robustness tested across models, prompts, and repeated runs.

A missed disclosure…

may reward an answer with incomplete warnings. If scores like these filter synthetic data, incomplete or unsupported examples can stay in a training set.

A false alarm…

Errors point in different directions

GPT-4o leans toward missed disclosures; Nano, Luna, and Sonnet lean toward false alarms. The counts motivate targeted expert review — they do not establish which judge is safest.

Takeaway When LLJ scores guide model selection or synthetic-data filtering, evaluation errors can propagate into future models and clinical decisions; validation and clinical oversight should therefore scale with the consequences of those uses.
04 The protocol

Six steps that put clinicians where they matter most

Rather than scoring every output, clinicians define and validate the evaluator: they write a task-specific codebook, annotate a calibration sample, and adjudicate uncertain or consequential disagreements. The LLJ then scores the remaining outputs — only within the scope that validation supports.

1Define

Define the evaluation construct and codebook

Before prompting any judge, specify what quality means for the task: dimensions, rating scales, examples, and exclusion criteria — including how to treat diagnosis aliases, ICD-10 specificity, unsupported findings, and acceptable paraphrases.

Domain experts + researchers
2Annotate

Build a human-annotated validation set

Sample outputs across quality levels, task types, and expected edge cases. Experts annotate independently with the codebook; report inter-annotator agreement and resolve meaningful disagreements into a reference label or an explicit “ambiguous” category.

≥ 2 qualified experts
3Design

Design the LLJ prompt from the human codebook

Mirror the codebook’s criteria, scales, decision rules, and allowed evidence. Ask for criterion-specific judgments instead of “is this answer good?”, cite the supporting source span, and avoid judges from the generator’s model family.

Researchers
4Validate

Validate LLJ–expert alignment before scaling

Report judge–human and human–human agreement per criterion. Stress-test runs, prompt variants, and judge models; probe order, format, scale, and verbosity effects; and check whether model rankings or filtering decisions change.

Researchers
5Analyze

Conduct clinical error analysis

Classify confirmed errors by type — missed safety issues, unsupported claims, important omissions, over-penalized aliases, overvalued fluency — and their frequency. Unresolved high-severity failures limit how the judge may be used.

Clinicians
6Report

Report and release evaluation artifacts

Publish the codebook, annotation procedure and qualifications, agreement and adjudication, prompt, judge version, run count, alignment report, and error analysis — together with the judge’s intended role and risk level.

Researchers
05 Readiness levels

Assurance proportional to the cost of being wrong

The evidence a judge needs depends on how its scores will be used. Each level states what an LLJ may be used for, the minimum assurance required, and the restrictions that apply.

L0
Exploratory
Permitted use

Error analysis, prompt comparison, hypothesis generation.

Minimum assurance
  • Report criteria, prompt, judge version, settings
  • Report run-to-run variability
  • State that the judge is unvalidated
No safety, superiority, or deployment claims.
L1
Validated reuse
Permitted use

Reuse within a benchmark’s validated scope, or a low-risk task that is easy to check.

Minimum assurance
  • Confirm task, population, criterion, judge, and prompt are still in scope
  • Report repeatability
  • Add expert review if the scope or conclusion changes
L2
Benchmark / model selection
Permitted use

Building benchmarks, ranking models, release decisions.

Minimum assurance
  • Expert codebook
  • Multi-clinician reference on a sample
  • Pre-specified margin against expert–expert agreement
  • Prompt, run, and judge stress tests
  • ≥ 95% bootstrap winner stability
  • Adjudication of high-severity cases
L3
Real-world use
Permitted use

Procurement, deployment, workflow decisions, safety-critical filtering.

Minimum assurance
Everything in L2, plus
  • Prospective or external validation
  • Audit trails and monitoring
  • Revalidation
  • Human override
Unresolved high-severity failures block use.
Lower consequenceHigher consequence of an incorrect evaluation

Requirements scale with intended use rather than study context. The numerical gates are provisional starting points, not universal standards.

06 Scaling responsibly

Scalable — but not automatically cheaper

LLJs do not inherently reduce total evaluation cost. The hybrid design spends clinician effort on calibration and escalation instead of on every output — and adds its own costs for inference, testing, and monitoring.
